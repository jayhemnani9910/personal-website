  keyDecisions:
    - decision: "Stacking rather than a single tuned model or simple voting"
      reasoning: "On a small, noisy dataset different classifiers make different mistakes. Stacking lets a meta-learner learn how to combine them from data, which is more flexible than hard or soft voting and was the hypothesis the paper set out to test."
      alternatives: "One well-tuned model (simpler, but leaves the complementary strengths of others unused); majority voting (combines models but cannot learn how to weight them)."
    - decision: "Tune the base models before stacking"
      reasoning: "A stack is only as good as its members. Running RandomizedSearchCV on five of the base classifiers first means the ensemble combines reasonable models rather than carrying weak defaults, and randomized search keeps tuning cheap across five models. Naive Bayes has little to tune and keeps a fixed var_smoothing."
      alternatives: "Stack untuned defaults (weaker base learners); exhaustive GridSearchCV (far more expensive across five models for little gain here)."
    - decision: "Evaluate on a fixed held-out split and report it honestly"
      reasoning: "With 768 rows the result is sensitive to the split, so fixing the random_state keeps it reproducible. The stack's test accuracy is reported as it is."
      alternatives: "Report only the best-looking number (misleading on small data); skip a held-out set (no honest generalization estimate)."

A stacking ensemble for early diabetes prediction on the PIMA Indians dataset (768 patients, 8 clinical features), co-authored and published at IEEE AIMV 2021. Six base classifiers (Gaussian Naive Bayes, Random Forest, Decision Tree, SVM, an ANN and Logistic Regression, all but Naive Bayes tuned with randomized search) are combined as the level-0 estimators of a scikit-learn StackingClassifier (cv=4) whose Random Forest meta-learner makes the final prediction. The whole pipeline, data through evaluation, lives in one notebook.
