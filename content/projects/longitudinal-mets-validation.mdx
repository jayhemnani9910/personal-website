challenge: "What happens when you take a risk score out of the country it was built in? RMRS and the B9 decision tree were developed and internally validated on Korean cohorts, and my literature scan found nobody testing them on long-term mortality in a US population. Doing that fairly is the hard part. NHANES is a complex survey, not a simple sample. People die of other things before the cause you're studying. And the tree was published as a figure and a ruleset, so it has to be rebuilt faithfully before it can be judged at all."
solution:
  - "I wrote all five scores in R from the source papers, each with unit tests: RMRS (a triangular-areal-similarity method), the B9 tree, the ACC/AHA Pooled Cohort Equations, Framingham 2008 and FINDRISC."
  - "The B9 tree is rebuilt in full, all 22 leaves from the paper's supplementary ruleset, and run with its native Korean feature scaling."
  - "The data is NHANES 1999 to 2018 linked to the 2019 Mortality File: 17,031 adults for all-cause deaths, and the 13,836 from cycles with cause-of-death coding for cardiovascular and diabetes-related deaths."
  - "Someone who dies of a heart attack can't later die of diabetes, so the models are survey-weighted Fine-Gray competing-risks and Cox fits. On top of that sit time-dependent AUC, decision curves, and a 500-replicate PSU-cluster bootstrap for the confidence intervals."
  - "To ask why a score falls short, an XGBoost ceiling shows how much signal the same inputs carry, and a CART refit on a held-out NHANES split tests the tree's Korean calibration."
impact:
  - "For cardiovascular and all-cause mortality the clinical equations clearly win. At 9.5 years Framingham 2008 reaches AUC 0.858 and 0.810, and RMRS reaches 0.660 and 0.600."
  - "For diabetes-related mortality it's close: RMRS 0.752 vs FINDRISC 0.770 at 14.5 years, statistically indistinguishable. Non-inferiority was not established at the -0.05 margin. RMRS still adds a little on top of FINDRISC (NRI and IDI confidence intervals exclude zero)."
  - "The rebuilt tree holds its own: RMRS stays ahead of it across the cohort, but only modestly. On a held-out split it reaches 0.764 for diabetes-related mortality, above RMRS at 0.735, and a US refit reaches 0.83. That refit is exploratory: one split, trained on metabolic syndrome instead of mortality."
  - "The limits: the manuscript is a draft, unpublished. The study protocol is written and in the repo, but it's retrospective, not a timestamped registration. There are only 77 diabetes-related deaths, the headline AUCs are unweighted, the cohort is the fasting subsample, and the tree's leaf risks were read off a published figure, so they're approximate."
  - "renv and uv pin the environments, and a Makefile rebuilds the cohort and result tables from raw NHANES. The bootstrap is its own, much slower target."

  keyDecisions:
    - decision: "Test the published scores as written, don't re-fit"
      reasoning: "A score re-fit on new data almost always looks good, and that tells you nothing about whether it travels. Testing the original on an independent population is what answers the question."
      alternatives: "Re-fit on NHANES (inflated and circular); report only the favourable outcome (cherry-picking)."
    - decision: "Survey-weighted competing risks, not plain Cox on raw data"
      reasoning: "NHANES is a complex survey, and deaths from one cause compete with deaths from others. Survey weights and Fine-Gray models handle both, and the PSU-cluster bootstrap respects how the sample was drawn."
      alternatives: "Unweighted Cox (ignores the design and the competing risks); logistic regression at one horizon (throws away time)."
    - decision: "Add an XGBoost ceiling and a CART refit to look for the why"
      reasoning: "A gap in AUC doesn't tell you what to do next. The ceiling shows what the inputs can carry: for diabetes-related mortality, 0.772 from the five metabolic-syndrome components alone. The refit points at the tree's Korean calibration over the tree method, though it's suggestive, not confirmed."
      alternatives: "Report the AUC gap and stop (no mechanism to act on)."

        weighted_finegray_hr <- function(df, score, time_col, status_col, ...) {
          # Geskus weights: survival::finegray() builds the weighted risk set
          fg <- survival::finegray(
            survival::Surv(.time, .status_f) ~ .score + .cluster, data = df
          )
          # subdistribution hazard via weighted Cox on the Fine-Gray data
          survival::coxph(
            survival::Surv(fgstart, fgstop, fgstatus) ~ .score,
            weights = fgwt, data = fg, ...
          )
        }

Most of the work here went into being fair to somebody else's score: rebuilding it from the paper, running it on a population it never saw, and handling the survey design and competing deaths properly. The twist is in the decision tree, which looked weak in a partial rebuild and held its own once all 22 leaves were in place. It's solo work, from the R pipeline to the LaTeX, and you can rerun it from raw NHANES.
